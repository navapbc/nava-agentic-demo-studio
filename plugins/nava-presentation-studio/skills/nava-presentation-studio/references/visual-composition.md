# Visual composition

## Compose around the point

Choose one dominant visual or typographic gesture for each slide. Supporting material should explain or qualify it. Start with hierarchy, not containers.

Use visual weight deliberately:

- large scale for the conclusion or evidence the audience should remember;
- proximity for items that belong together;
- contrast for the tension or change;
- whitespace to separate ideas and control pace;
- alignment to make relationships legible;
- repetition for a true series, not as a default layout system.

Equal columns communicate peers. A grid of cards communicates a catalog. Use those meanings intentionally.

## Match evidence to form

- **Change over time:** line, slope, timeline, or before-and-after comparison.
- **Magnitude:** bar, big number with context, or scaled comparison.
- **Mechanism:** simple editable diagram with labels close to the parts.
- **Product reality:** a legible screenshot with the relevant region emphasized by crop, scale, or a short caption.
- **Human experience:** one quote or documentary image with enough context to be credible.
- **Strategic possibility:** a horizon, landscape, or relationship map with a clearly marked present state.

Do not use a screenshot as wallpaper. Do not shrink a dashboard until its evidence is unreadable. Crop or rebuild the necessary evidence.

## Vary the rhythm

Balance dense proof slides with sparse synthesis slides. A useful sequence may alternate:

- visual evidence;
- explanation;
- synthesis;
- another piece of evidence;
- a sparse reveal.

Avoid repeating the same three-column composition across consecutive slides. Reuse a layout family only when the slides form a deliberate series.

For a template-led deck, make a rhythm map before authoring. For every slide, record:

- background or color-field treatment;
- dominant evidence form or typographic gesture;
- density level;
- composition family;
- the contrast or continuity it creates with the previous slide.

Use the template's archetype library as a vocabulary. A clean branded deck should not collapse every claim into a white title-and-body slide merely because that layout is easy to populate. For decks longer than roughly ten slides, the story will usually need several distinct archetypes—such as cover, image-led proof, big number, process, comparison, sparse statement, quote, reveal, and decision—without treating variety as a quota.

Create visual drama through evidence, scale, crop, contrast, and pacing. Do not add color, cards, icons, or decorative geometry only to make a slide feel busier.

Deliberately reserve a distinct treatment for the deck's reveal or strategic climax. It should be recognizable in the contact sheet before its text is readable.

## Use real artifacts

Prefer real screenshots, evaluation traces, product recordings, field photos, and source charts when they exist. They make progress believable and often carry the brand more naturally than decorative illustration.

When using product artifacts:

- remove unrelated chrome and personal information;
- keep text and controls legible;
- preserve aspect ratio;
- add only the annotation needed to direct attention;
- state whether the artifact is a prototype, test, or production surface.

## Edit visible copy

Aim for one title, one short explanatory block, and only the labels needed by the visual. Put supporting detail in speaker notes. Shorten before shrinking type.

Use plain language. Avoid vague headings, slogans, excessive punctuation, passive voice, and decorative labels. A title may name the subject directly or state a supported takeaway. It should not narrate the presenter's actions.

## Use measurable spacing

Treat spacing as part of the argument, not cleanup after the fact.

- Keep at least 24 points of safe margin from slide edges; 30–36 points is usually calmer for executive slides.
- Leave 12–18 points between a title and subtitle or standfirst.
- Leave 8–12 points between a module heading and its own description, and 20–32 points between separate modules.
- Give filled shapes 14–20 points of internal padding when the tool permits it.
- Do not place distinct hierarchy levels closer than 8 points. If two blocks feel cramped, shorten the copy or enlarge the region before reducing type.
- A blank region larger than roughly one quarter of the slide needs a visible job: pacing, contrast, a reveal, or a dominant image. Otherwise rebalance the composition.
- Never leave a bullet glyph, icon label, callout, or caption without visible text.

Reserve the title region before positioning the main visual. Measure from the last rendered title line, not the nominal text-box bottom: wrapped text can exceed its box. Leave roughly 18–24 points before unrelated content. When the title changes, recheck the diagram and its labels together rather than moving only its background.

Template placeholders are starting geometry. A narrow stage label is not a suitable body column by default. Widen or regroup the content while preserving the template's hierarchy; check natural wrapping at presentation size.

Put timeline and chart strokes in their own visual lanes. Lines may meet deliberate node markers, but should not cross narrative copy. Leave space for full text height above or below the line, including wrapped captions.

For quantitative progress, give the result visual priority: a large reduction figure plus a small paired bar or slope often communicates more clearly than several equally sized cost labels. If workloads differ, use independent comparisons and label their scales; do not connect them into one apparent cost curve.

When the product itself is the evidence, make it legible. A central screenshot should normally occupy at least half the slide or receive a full-slide treatment. Use a detail crop only when the audience does not need the surrounding workflow.

## Full-deck review

Check the contact sheet for pace, repetition, unexplained empty regions, and an obvious visual climax. Then inspect each slide for legibility, object fit, image crop, text wrapping, source notes, spacing between hierarchy levels, empty bullets, and brand consistency. A polished slide that weakens the sequence still needs revision.

Treat a contact sheet dominated by one background color, one title position, or one text-first composition as a design defect unless the deck is intentionally austere. The correction is usually a better evidence form or template archetype, not additional decoration inside the same layout.
