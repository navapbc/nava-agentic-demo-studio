# Nava presentation standards

## Lead with the public-interest consequence

Make the audience understand who benefits, what friction changes, and why the work matters before asking them to absorb implementation detail. Use technical language only when it changes the decision.

## Let evidence carry the brand

Prefer real product surfaces, research artifacts, service-delivery context, evaluated results, and clear diagrams over decorative illustration. A Nava deck should feel grounded in the work.

When a current Nava template or reference deck is available, derive typography, color, spacing, logo treatment, and recurring motifs from that file. Do not treat remembered hex values or old slide layouts as authoritative brand guidance.

## Tell a progress story, not a feature inventory

Organize progress as a sequence of problems resolved, capabilities gained, and choices opened. Show the constraint that motivated each piece of work. If several workstreams combine into a reusable capability, make that synthesis explicit.

Make enabling work visible when it changes what the team can prove. Evaluation harnesses, session-level instrumentation, provenance checks, and architecture changes belong in the story when they are the reason later cost or quality claims are credible.

## Riverside revision lessons

The Riverside deck revisions exposed several transferable lessons:

- Replace abstract prescriptions with the user problem or strategic tension.
- Show the product and the proof instead of describing both in paragraphs.
- Reduce the number of equally weighted ideas on each slide.
- Make the title and the visual work together to state the point.
- Use the main deck for the clean narrative and move supporting detail to an appendix.
- Make progress concrete with dated artifacts, tested metrics, and visible caveats.
- Translate internal project vocabulary for partner and leadership audiences.
- Preserve the denominator. Do not turn a session-level pilot cost, a per-application experiment, and marginal on-device API cost into one unlabeled reduction curve.
- Give central product artifacts a full or majority-slide treatment rather than leaving them as small decorative screenshots.
- Use the reviewer gates to catch cramped hierarchy, unexplained empty regions, empty bullets, and repeated compositions before delivery.

These are judgment rules, not a layout template. Do not copy Riverside-specific content, metrics, screenshots, or partner information into another deck.

## Responsible claims

- Mark prototypes and pilots as prototypes and pilots.
- Do not imply production readiness from a successful demo.
- Keep privacy, accessibility, and human-decision boundaries visible when they are material.
- Use only the minimum partner or participant detail needed to support the point.
- Keep confidential or identifying information out of screenshots and public examples.
